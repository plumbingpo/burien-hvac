Think of a heat pump as an air conditioner that can work in both directions. In summer it pulls heat out of your home; in winter a reversing valve flips the refrigerant flow, and the same compressor draws heat from the outdoor air and brings it inside. Because it moves heat instead of creating it, a heat pump can deliver two to three times more heat energy than the electricity it uses, which is a big reason homeowners in Burien, WA are making the switch as older systems wear out. Today's cold-climate models, built around variable-speed inverter compressors, keep producing useful heat well below freezing, a real leap past the heat pumps of twenty years ago.

The design phase decides whether a heat pump lives up to its promise. Unlike a furnace or an AC alone, it must handle both the cooling load and the heating load, so we calculate each one and locate the balance point, the outdoor temperature below which the unit needs help. From there we match compressor staging to your climate and ducts and design the backup heat: electric strips that stage in only when needed, or a dual-fuel pairing where a gas furnace handles the coldest stretches. Electrical capacity and duct airflow get verified too, because the lower supply temperature of a heat pump only feels right with enough air moving.

Installation follows the same discipline as every Burien HVAC system: line set pressure-tested and evacuated to a micron-verified vacuum, charge weighed in and confirmed with superheat and subcooling, the outdoor unit elevated on a stand where winter defrost drainage demands it, condensate handled properly, and the thermostat configured so staging, backup lockouts, and defrost behave the way the engineering intended. Then we commission in both modes, heating and cooling, and record the readings. Why Professional Heat Pump Installation Matters

Heat pumps punish installation shortcuts more than any other residential system because they work both sides of the year. A charge that is slightly off hurts twice, in cooling efficiency all summer and heating capacity all winter. A balance point never calculated means electric backup strips quietly carrying the house at several times the operating cost while the homeowner wonders why the bills did not drop. A thermostat left on defaults can run backup heat when the compressor alone would do, or fight the defrost cycle. And an outdoor unit set flat on the ground in a real winter sits in its own refrozen defrost water until the coil is a block of ice.

We design and install to prevent every one of those problems. Loads are calculated for heating and cooling, equipment is selected for its actual capacity at your winter design temperature, backup heat is staged and locked out properly, the outdoor unit is raised for defrost drainage, and the charge is weighed and verified instead of guessed. Commissioning covers both modes, including a check of the reversing valve and a full defrost cycle. Refrigerant handling is federally regulated, and inverter equipment brings electronics that reward attention to detail. How to Prevent Heat Pump Installation Problems

Taking preventive measures can help you avoid costly heating and cooling problems down the road. Here are our expert recommendations:

  • Book twice-yearly maintenance so charge and defrost stay verified in both modes
  • Avoid deep thermostat setbacks, which can call on costly backup heat during recovery
  • Keep a regular filter schedule so the system has enough air in summer and winter
  • Keep an eye on winter bills; a sharp increase usually means the backup heat is running too much
  • Light frost and an occasional defrost hiss are normal in winter, but solid ice calls for service
  • Give the outdoor unit open space all year, free of drifts and overgrowth
  • Gently hose off the outdoor coil every spring before cooling season
