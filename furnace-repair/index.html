Every time your thermostat calls for heat, the furnace works through a fixed checklist. The inducer motor starts and proves draft by closing a pressure switch, the hot surface igniter heats up, the gas valve opens, the burners light, and the flame sensor confirms there is fire. Only after all of that does the blower begin sending warm air into the house. Finding the step that fails is most of the job. Around Burien, WA, the usual failure points are a flame sensor fouled with oxide, an igniter cracked by years of heating and cooling, a pressure switch that stays open because of a weak inducer or a clogged condensate trap on a high-efficiency unit, and control boards with burned relays. Any one of them halts the startup, and from the hallway they all look alike.

Rather than guess, Burien HVAC tests the sequence stage by stage. We read igniter resistance, measure flame signal in microamps, check that the pressure switch responds to real inducer draft, confirm voltage at the gas valve, and test the blower and its capacitor. Because this is a fuel-burning appliance, the safety basics come with every visit: the heat exchanger gets inspected, gas connections get leak-tested, and draft gets verified. You cannot tell a furnace problem from a furnace hazard by looking at the thermostat. Before we leave, temperature rise is measured against the data plate so the fire and the airflow are balanced as the manufacturer intended.

We work on natural gas, propane, and electric furnaces from every major brand, both conventional 80 percent units and condensing high-efficiency models with their traps, drains, and dual pressure switches. The truck stock covers the parts that actually fail: igniters, flame sensors, pressure switches, capacitors, inducer assemblies, and blower motors, so most no-heat calls end with heat the same visit. When to Call for Furnace Repair in Burien

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you time, money, and prevent significant damage to your property. These are the signals our technicians see most often when a problem is underway:

  • Loud booms at startup caused by delayed ignition
  • Burners light, run a few seconds, then shut down repeatedly
  • Soot, rust, or moisture near the furnace cabinet or vent pipe
  • The furnace clicks and glows but the burners never light
  • Squealing or grinding from the blower compartment
  • Frequent on-off cycling that never reaches the thermostat setting
  • Yellow, wavering burner flames where they should be sharp blue
  • The blower runs constantly but the air never warms

Why Hire a Professional for Furnace Repair?

Gas, open flame, high voltage, and a flue share one cabinet in a furnace, and the most dangerous failure is the one you cannot see: a damaged heat exchanger that lets combustion gases into the air the blower circulates. Every morning that delayed ignition keeps booming at startup, that exchanger takes more stress. The diagnostic side has its own pitfalls. When a furnace trips its high limit, the switch itself may be bad, or the furnace may be protecting itself from a clogged filter or a failing blower. Swapping the switch in that second case removes a safety device and leaves the actual problem in place.

Our experienced technicians look at every symptom in the context of the whole furnace. We measure flame signal instead of assuming it, which keeps us from replacing a healthy sensor while a cracked igniter or weak gas valve goes unnoticed. Draft is confirmed at the pressure switch and at the flue. Temperature rise is checked against the data plate, so airflow issues are caught before they shorten the life of the heat exchanger. The gas side is leak-tested with instruments, never with a quick sniff. What you get is a furnace proven safe over a full cycle, plus an honest opinion if the unit is getting close to the point where replacing it beats repairing it again.

How to Prevent Furnace Repair Problems

Smart maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Follow these tips from our experienced technicians:

  • Schedule service each fall to catch worn igniters and dirty sensors before cold weather arrives
  • Change the filter monthly during heating season; it is the cheapest furnace part you will ever buy
  • Give the furnace room to breathe, since a crowded utility space starves the burners of air
  • Listen at light-off; clean ignition is quiet, and booming means the burners need service
  • Test your CO detectors monthly and make sure each floor has one
  • If you have a high-efficiency furnace, check that the condensate drain is flowing freely
  • Keep storage, lint, and especially chlorine-based products away from the furnace closet
